## Responses

- **Feature with no strategic answer (why is this a 12-week priority?):** Monetization features (premium tiers, paywalls). If an engineer asked "why now?", I couldn't answer. I have no evidence of willingness to pay, no pricing signal, and no retention baseline showing users get enough value to pay for. Monetizing before retention is proven risks both churn and revenue. It's only a priority once we know which job users hire the app for and that they come back.

- **Feature that looks "correct" but has zero strategic weight:** Siri / Google Assistant integration. It's what apps in this category are "supposed" to have, but I can't point to a user who asked for it. I also can't show it moves activation, retention, or revenue. It would cost real engineering time (platform review, maintenance) for an unproven benefit. It's a "me-too" feature, not a strategic one.

- **V3 vs your Step 1 baseline — what changed, and was it your PM knowledge that forced usefulness?:** The AI didn't get smarter. V3 was better because my input got better. In Step 1 the AI had little to go on, so it produced a generic feature list. By V3 I had supplied JTBD, effort/impact scoring, and retention context. That gave it constraints and criteria for ranking, so it could prioritize instead of just listing. The more context and decision criteria the AI has, the more specific and defensible the output. The PM's framing turned a feature dump into a strategy.

- **Key takeaway — how does this prove the PM's role is to think while the AI's role is to execute?:** AI is strong at structuring, synthesizing, and generating at speed, but it can't decide what matters. Only the PM knows the business goal, the user's real problem, and the trade-offs. The PM supplies the "why" (goals, JTBD, constraints, success metrics), and the AI turns it into the "what" (a structured, ranked map). Bad input gives confident-sounding but strategically empty output. Good thinking up front is what makes AI output useful.
